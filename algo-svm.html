Support Vector Machine (SVM)

"Is this order suspicious?"

In plain words

It sorts orders into normal and suspicious, so a person can check the risky ones first.

Think of it like this: A security guard who draws a line between ordinary orders and unusual ones. Orders that fall on the risky side get a flag.

Try the demo

  1. Move the "Decision threshold" slider.
  2. A higher threshold means fewer, stricter flags. A lower one means more flags.
  3. Look at the chart. Flagged orders are the ones to check.

Words to know

Flag
A warning that an order should be checked by a person.
Threshold
How strict the guard is. Higher means stricter.
False alarm
A safe order that was flagged by mistake.
Missed fraud
A risky order that was not flagged.

What it is

Support Vector Machine draws the clearest possible dividing line between two groups (normal order vs. suspicious order), keeping the margin as wide as possible from both groups. It provides robust decision boundaries in high-dimensional feature spaces.

Why Allonola uses it

Because Allonola offers cash on delivery and third-party courier dispatch, fraudulent orders (such as fake buyers ordering bulk expensive electronics with freshly minted accounts) cost significant capital in wasted courier fees. SVM screens thousands of transactions in real time, routing high-risk flags to fraud analysts. This operational safeguard directly advances Operational Goal #3: keeping fraud-related losses below 0.5% of GMV. In parallel, SVM text classification automatically assigns new seller listings to correct catalog categories.

How it works

The algorithm identifies an optimal separating hyperplane that maximizes the geometric margin between normal and fraudulent data points. The training examples that sit right on the edge of the margin are the support vectors.

Hyperplane Decision: w · x - b = 0 Maximize Margin: 2 / ||w|| subject to y_i(w · x_i - b) ≥ 1 Kernel Mapping (RBF): K(x, x') = exp(-γ ||x - x'||²)

When raw data cannot be separated by a straight line, kernel functions (such as Radial Basis Function / RBF) project inputs into higher-dimensional feature spaces where linear separation becomes feasible. Product text descriptions are vectorized into TF-IDF numerical values, and class weighting penalties compensate for the severe rarity of fraud in real transaction streams.

Test-set results and interpretation

Highest-scoring demo order Score 0.41 Order O-1053 (confirmed fraud) is the top score among the 60 demo orders
Fraud Test Set ROC-AUC 0.78 800 held-out orders (66 fraud = 8.3%): F1 = 0.35
Flagged at default cut-off 133 of 800 35 of 66 frauds caught (precision 0.26, recall 0.53)

Interpretation

Accuracy alone is misleading: a model that never flags fraud would score about 92% accuracy on this test set while catching none. F1-score and ROC-AUC evaluate performance on the rare fraud class. The 98 false alarms at the default cut-off are a deliberate trade-off, because analysts can review flagged orders before dispatch.

Business Impact

At the default cut-off, analysts review 133 flagged orders out of 800 and catch 35 of 66 frauds (53%). Lowering the threshold catches more fraud at the cost of more reviews; the cut-off is a business decision.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

  • Excels in high-dimensional feature spaces with many sparse variables.
  • Kernel trick enables effective non-linear separation without manual feature engineering.
  • Robust against overfitting because decision boundaries depend only on support vectors.
  • Class weights allow effective tuning against heavily imbalanced fraud distributions.

Limitations

  • Computationally slow to train on massive datasets with hundreds of thousands of rows.
  • Hyperparameters (regularization constant C and kernel gamma) require careful cross-validation tuning.
  • Does not directly produce calibrated probabilistic confidence scores without Platt scaling.
  • Can be sensitive to extreme noise points near the margin boundary.
